## Diseña tu ciudad: Digital Literacy Is Not Enough

During a usability testing exercise, our team evaluated Diseña tu ciudad. We divided participants into two groups: 18–23 year olds and 40–60 year olds. Our assumption was that the younger group would perform better due to their digital familiarity. The results proved that we were wrong, only one participant from each group completed the task successfully. The younger group abandoned after three failed attempts with the INE recognition feature; the older group also failed, but persisted longer before giving up. 

The key insight was not about the technical bug, it was about motivation. As Travis (2010) said, for usability tests to reveal real problems, participants must actually want to complete the task. Group 1 had the technical ability but not the motivation. Voting on park construction decisions was not relevant enough to justify repeated failures. Group 2 persisted longer precisely because the topic, public spaces in their city, was more meaningful to them. Digital literacy tells you what a user can do. Motivation tells you what they will actually do. 

This applies directly to the CGRH project. Our users have strong intrinsic motivation, they need their CFDI for bank procedures, their savings fund for financial decisions. That motivation is a design advantage, but not an unlimited one. The lesson is clear: motivation determines how much friction a user will tolerate, but no amount of motivation compensates for a broken flow, an expired link, or an error message that does not explain what to do next.
