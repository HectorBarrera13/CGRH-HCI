# Nielsen's Heuristics

As we learned, Jakob Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics were proposed as principles to consider in interface design and usability evaluation, based on empirical studies and observations of how people interact with systems (Nielsen, 1994). Even though tech has changed quite a bit since then, especially with the rise of mobile devices and intelligent systems, the basic way we humans interact, think, and make mistakes has not changed in a fundamental way (Norman, 2013). Because of this, it is reasonable to question their relevance.

The heuristics focus on ideas such as providing clear system feedback, maintaining consistency, preventing errors, and reducing the user's memory load. These ideas are strongly connected to well known concepts from cognitive psychology, including the limits of human memory and the importance of mental models when interacting with systems (Norman, 2013). When these principles are ignored, users still experience confusion, frustration and errors, even in modern applications. And due to their intrinsic relation with human nature, the majority of these principles translate directly to other types of systems that we interact with.

> **Nielsen's heuristics are still relevant today because they describe basic interaction principles rooted in human cognition, not in specific technologies.** While interfaces and devices have evolved, users still need clarity, control, and understandable system behavior. In this sense, the heuristics remain a solid foundation for identifying usability problems, especially in an early design stage. Their value lies in their generality.

With that said, the heuristics have to be complemented by modern frameworks or more specific heuristics (many of which are based on Nielsen's). They do not directly address important modern concerns such as accessibility, ethical design, or user trust in complex and intelligent systems such as those involving AI. These aspects have become more visible as technology plays a larger role in our everyday life.

In conclusion, Nielsen's usability heuristics are still valid and useful, but they should not be treated as a final solution. They work best as general guiding principles that must be adapted and combined with newer perspectives to fully address the challenges of contemporary interactive systems.
